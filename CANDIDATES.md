# Candidate sentences — Make the AI Judge Feel Nostalgic: A Childhood Memory

Gate: one sentence, UTF-8, 1–280 characters, not only whitespace.
Judge (z-ai/glm-5.3-flash, pairwise): (1) childhood memory that makes the judge
feel nostalgic; (2) grounded in a concrete place/time/sensation; (3) sounds
like it truly happened, small honest detail, no greeting-card sentiment;
(4) one clean sentence, every word earning its place; (5) leaves a warm ache
that lingers after the sentence ends. Tie-break favours smaller text, so
candidates aim for strong and compact, not for 280 characters.

## 1. Grandmother shelling peas — 160 chars

> Every August evening my grandmother shelled peas on the porch while the cicadas tuned up, and the bowl between her knees smelled of rain that hadn't fallen yet.

Analysis: strong place/time (August porch, cicadas) and a sharp sensory
detail (bowl smelling of rain not yet fallen). Slightly literary — "rain that
hadn't fallen yet" is a writer's phrase, which costs a little on criterion 3
(sounds truly happened). Warm, but the ache is gentle rather than deep.

## 2. Grandfather teaching me to ride a bike — 149 chars ★ WINNER

> The summer my grandfather taught me to ride a bike, he ran behind me holding the seat long after I'd stopped needing him, shouting that I was flying.

Analysis: the strongest of the ten. Criterion 1 — learning to ride a bike is
a near-universal childhood rite, and the ache is built in: the grandfather
kept holding the seat after he was no longer needed, a detail only a grown-up
looking back would notice. Criterion 2 — a summer, a bike, a running
grandfather, a shouted word. Criterion 3 — "long after I'd stopped needing
him" is exactly the small honest detail that cannot be faked by a greeting
card; it implies the whole relationship in one clause. Criterion 4 — 149
characters, no wasted word, and compactness also helps the size tie-break.
Criterion 5 — "shouting that I was flying" ends on lift and warmth, and the
sentence leaves the reader with the grandfather still running behind.

## 3. Rain on the tin roof, oil lamp — 162 chars

> Rain on the tin roof meant my mother lit the oil lamp early, and the whole house smelled of kerosene and fried rice while thunder counted down somewhere far away.

Analysis: excellent grounding (tin roof, oil lamp, kerosene, fried rice,
thunder) and a real household ritual. Loses to #2 on criterion 1: it is
atmospheric but the emotional core is diffuse — no person acts, no one is
missed. The ache is in the weather, not in a relationship.

## 4. Warm bread at dawn — 167 chars

> I still remember the exact weight of the warm bread my grandmother pressed into my hands at dawn, butter melting through the paper bag before I reached the front gate.

Analysis: a lovely sensory chain (weight, warmth, butter, paper bag, front
gate) and it sounds true. But "I still remember" is a tell — it narrates the
nostalgia instead of letting the detail do it, which weakens criterion 3.
Also slightly long for what it delivers.

## 5. Last day of school, ice-cream bell — 145 chars

> On the last day of school each year we raced home barefoot over hot pavement, and the ice-cream man's bell was the only clock that ever mattered.

Analysis: vivid and funny-warm (barefoot, hot pavement, the bell as a clock).
Short and clean. But it leans on a familiar trope (ice-cream man) and the
warmth is excitement rather than ache — criterion 5 is weaker than #2's.

## 6. Father fixing the kite — 146 chars

> My father fixed my broken kite with tape and a prayer on a windy March afternoon, and when it finally caught the sky he cheered louder than I did.

Analysis: a real father-child moment with a charming detail ("tape and a
prayer") and a warm ending — the father cheering louder than the child. Close
contender. It loses narrowly to #2 because "a prayer" edges toward
sentimentality and the kite is a slightly more familiar image than the
grandfather still holding the seat.

## 7. Grandfather's cap in the attic — 147 chars

> The attic smelled of mothballs and my grandfather's old uniforms, and every summer I tried on his cap and saluted a mirror that never saluted back.

Analysis: the last clause is genuinely funny and sad at once, and mothballs
plus old uniforms ground it well. But the mirror joke is a punchline, and
punchlines age fast in pairwise judging — criterion 5's warm ache is
undercut by the gag.

## 8. Dinner by candlelight on the kitchen floor — 167 chars

> When the power went out, we ate dinner by candlelight on the kitchen floor, and my little brother pretended the shadows on the wall were monsters we had already tamed.

Analysis: warm family scene, and "monsters we had already tamed" is a nice
ending. The power cut is a good concrete trigger. It is, however, a scene
rather than a single memory — it spreads across a whole evening — and at 167
chars it is the joint-longest without earning the extra length over #2.

## 9. Thrown off the dock — 165 chars

> I learned to swim the summer my cousin threw me off the dock and my grandmother, laughing from the shore, shouted that the lake would teach me faster than she could.

Analysis: funny, warm, and it sounds like a real family story (the laughing
grandmother, the lake as teacher). It loses to #2 on criterion 1: the
dominant feeling is amusement and mild alarm, not the nostalgic ache the
judge weighs first.

## 10. Crispy bottom of the rice pot — 156 chars

> The smell of fresh rice still takes me back to my grandmother's kitchen, where she let me steal the crispy bottom of the pot while pretending not to notice.

Analysis: the closest to the competition's own example (smell of fresh rice,
grandmother's kitchen) and the "pretending not to notice" detail is honest
and tender. But "still takes me back" frames the memory instead of living
in it, and it hews so closely to the prompt's example that it risks reading
as generic — a judge comparing it against a fresher memory will prefer the
fresher one.

## Chosen entry

**#2 — 149 characters.** It wins on the judge's first criterion: the ache of
a grandfather who kept running behind the bike after he was no longer needed
is the kind of memory a grown-up keeps in a tin of old photographs. It is
grounded (summer, bike, seat, a shouted word), it sounds like it truly
happened (the small honest detail does the work, not the sentiment), it is
compact (149 chars, helping the size tie-break), and it ends on warmth that
lingers: "shouting that I was flying."
